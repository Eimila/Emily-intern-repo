# Working with Vulnerable Populations — Inclusive Design Reflection

## A first-person perspective

I'm approaching this reflection partly from my own experience, since I have ADHD myself. A lot of what Focus Bear is built to help with is very real to me: I often forget everyday tasks like brushing my teeth, I put off things like showering, I get distracted easily, and I tend to juggle too many things at once. One of the hardest parts isn't any single task — it's the constant sense of negative feedback, the feeling that you're always slightly failing at basic things that seem effortless for other people. That experience shapes how I think a productivity tool for people like me should feel: it should reduce that sense of failure, not add to it.

## Who are vulnerable populations, and what challenges do they face in digital spaces?

Vulnerable populations include neurodivergent people (such as those with ADHD or Autism), people with executive-functioning difficulties, and more broadly anyone who can be disadvantaged or overwhelmed by how digital products are designed. In digital spaces they may struggle with cluttered interfaces, too many choices, unpredictable navigation, sensory overload (bright colours, noise, animation), and language or flows that assume a level of focus and motivation they can't always summon. A design that's merely "busy" for a typical user can be genuinely blocking for them.

## Ethical considerations when designing for neurodivergent individuals

- Don't overwhelm the user — keep the UX simple and avoid cognitive overload.
- Respect sensory needs — be careful with intense colours, sounds, and animation.
- Communicate clearly and predictably, so the product feels safe and trustworthy.
- Avoid shame-based or punishing design; people already carry enough negative feedback.
- Give users control and flexibility rather than forcing one "correct" way to use the app.

## Making interactions and content more accessible for ADHD / Autism

- Use simple, direct language and short instructions.
- Keep navigation predictable and consistent, so users don't have to relearn things.
- Reduce cognitive load — one clear action at a time instead of many competing options.
- Break large tasks into small, manageable steps.
- Be encouraging and low-pressure in tone, so the experience builds momentum instead of guilt.

## Supporting neurodivergent team members

- Be clear and explicit in communication rather than relying on hints or assumptions.
- Respect different working styles and rhythms instead of expecting everyone to work the same way.
- Allow flexibility where possible, and give people clear structure and expectations.
- Assume good intent, and make it easy to ask for clarification without judgement.

## Reflection

### How can you adjust your communication style to be more inclusive?

- Be clear, concrete, and concise — say exactly what I mean rather than leaving things implied.
- Break information into small, digestible pieces instead of long dense blocks.
- Be patient and non-judgemental, especially when someone needs something repeated or clarified.
- Avoid a tone that could feel critical or shaming, since many neurodivergent people are already hard on themselves.
- Confirm understanding and follow up in writing, so nothing depends on perfect memory in the moment.

### Common UX or communication pitfalls that make Focus Bear less accessible

- Overloading screens with too much information or too many options at once.
- Unpredictable or inconsistent navigation that forces users to relearn flows.
- Punishing or guilt-inducing feedback (e.g. emphasising failure and broken streaks) that adds to negative self-talk.
- Language that's too long, vague, or complex.
- Sensory overload from bright colours, sudden sounds, or heavy animation.

### One practical change I can make in my work

When I build or work on features, I'll deliberately design for the "low-focus, low-motivation" moment rather than the ideal user — keeping flows simple, steps small, and language kind, so the app supports people exactly when they're struggling most, instead of assuming they're at their best.

## Task

### One design or communication improvement for Focus Bear

Make feedback more compassionate rather than punishing. For example, when a user misses a habit or breaks a streak, the app could respond in an encouraging, forgiving way ("Let's pick it back up today") instead of emphasising the failure. For users who already experience a lot of negative feedback in daily life, this small shift in tone can make the difference between opening the app again and abandoning it.

### A clear, patient, supportive response to a hypothetical struggling user

> Hi, thank you for reaching out — and honestly, what you're describing is really common, so you're not doing anything wrong. When everything feels like too much, it can help to forget the big picture for a moment and just pick one small thing to start with. You don't need to catch up on everything at once. Focus Bear is here to support you, not to judge you, and missing a day doesn't undo your progress. Would it help if we set up just one small, easy routine to rebuild a bit of momentum? We can take it one step at a time, at whatever pace works for you.
