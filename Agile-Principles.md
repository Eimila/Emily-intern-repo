## Module 1: Agile Principles and Frameworks

### Research and Learn

What is Agile, and how does it differ from traditional project management?

Agile is an iterative approach to building products where work is broken into small increments, delivered frequently, and adjusted based on feedback. Traditional project management, often called Waterfall, plans the entire project upfront in sequential phases (requirements, design, build, test, release) and only delivers a finished result at the end. Agile differs by embracing change throughout the project, delivering working software early and often, and relying on continuous collaboration with stakeholders rather than a fixed plan set in stone at the start.

What are the core values and principles of Agile?

The Agile Manifesto states four core values: individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over following a plan. The manifesto is backed by twelve principles, including satisfying customers through early and continuous delivery, welcoming changing requirements even late in development, delivering working software frequently, and maintaining a sustainable pace of work.

What are the differences between Scrum and Kanban, and when is each used?

Scrum organizes work into fixed-length sprints, usually one to four weeks, with defined roles such as Scrum Master and Product Owner, and ceremonies like sprint planning, daily stand-ups, sprint review, and retrospective. It works well for teams that benefit from a predictable cadence and a batch of committed work each sprint. Kanban, by contrast, has no fixed iterations. Work flows continuously through columns on a board, prioritization happens on an ongoing basis, and the main discipline is limiting work in progress. Kanban tends to suit teams with unpredictable or continuously arriving work, such as support, maintenance, or fast-moving product teams, where committing to a fixed two-week batch of work doesn't fit reality well.

## Reflection

What do you think are the biggest benefits and challenges of Agile?

The biggest benefit of Agile is that it shortens the feedback loop: teams ship smaller pieces of work, see how they land with real users, and course-correct quickly instead of discovering a mismatch after months of work. It also keeps priorities flexible, which suits products that need to adapt to a changing market. The biggest challenges are that Agile requires real discipline to work well; without consistent ceremonies and honest communication, it can devolve into unplanned, reactive work with no clear direction. It can also make long-term planning harder, since priorities shift often and it's easy to lose sight of the bigger roadmap if the team is too focused on the next task.

How can Agile principles help improve your role and responsibilities?

Applying Agile principles means breaking my own work into smaller, shippable increments rather than trying to complete large chunks before getting feedback. It also means treating my board status as a communication tool, updating it in real time so teammates always know what I'm working on and what's blocked, and staying open to reprioritizing when something more urgent comes in rather than being rigid about a personal task order.

## Task

Summarize the main differences between Scrum and Kanban in your own words.

Scrum works in fixed sprints with defined roles and a set of regular ceremonies, and the team commits to a specific batch of work at the start of each sprint. Kanban has no sprints or fixed roles; work items flow continuously through the board, get pulled in based on priority and capacity, and the team limits how much can be in progress at once instead of committing to a fixed batch. Scrum suits predictable, plannable work; Kanban suits continuous, unpredictable work.

Identify one Agile principle you think will be most useful in your work.

The principle of delivering working software frequently, in the smallest useful increments. Shipping small pieces early means I get feedback and catch issues sooner, instead of investing a lot of effort into something before finding out it needs to change.
