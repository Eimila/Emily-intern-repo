# Cyber Security Guidelines

## Research & Learn

**Common cyber security threats in a remote work environment**
Remote work increases exposure to phishing emails and messages, unsecured or public Wi-Fi networks, weak or reused passwords, unlocked devices left unattended in public or shared spaces, and malware from untrusted downloads. Because remote workers often use personal networks and devices, there's also more risk of company data being accessed outside of controlled, monitored environments.

**Best practices for keeping devices and accounts secure**
Use strong, unique passwords for every account, store them in a password manager rather than memorizing or reusing them, enable two-factor authentication (2FA) wherever it's offered, keep operating systems and apps updated, and avoid installing software from untrusted sources.

**Why locking your computer when away from your desk matters**
An unlocked device gives anyone nearby full access to logged-in accounts, company systems, and sensitive files — no password needed. Locking the screen (or closing the laptop) whenever you step away removes that window of opportunity, even in a trusted space like an office or library.

**Handling phishing attempts and suspicious links**
Never click links or open attachments from unexpected or unfamiliar senders. Check the sender's actual email address rather than just the display name, hover over links to preview the real destination before clicking, and treat urgency ("act now," "your account will be suspended") as a red flag. If something looks suspicious, report it and verify through a separate, trusted channel rather than replying or clicking.

**What makes a strong password, and why use a password manager**
A strong password is long (ideally 12+ characters), random rather than guessable, and unique to that one account — reusing passwords means one breach compromises every account that shares it. A password manager makes this practical by generating and storing strong, unique passwords for every account so you don't have to remember them, while also making it fast to unlock accounts via biometrics (fingerprint/Face ID) instead of typing.

**Why two-factor authentication matters**
2FA means that even if a password is stolen or guessed, an attacker still can't log in without a second factor (a code, a passkey, or biometric confirmation on a trusted device). It should be enabled on any account that holds sensitive data or system access — email, code repositories, cloud/admin accounts, and any company tools — since these are the accounts an attacker would target first.

## Reflection

**What security measures do you currently follow, and where can you improve?**
I already have strong, unique passwords on my work accounts, 2FA enabled on GitHub, all passwords managed through Apple's built-in password manager with fingerprint unlock, and both my computer and phone set to auto-lock. Where I can still improve is extending 2FA beyond GitHub to my other work accounts (email, Focus Bear account) so a single compromised password can't be enough to get in anywhere.

**How can you make secure behaviour a habit rather than an afterthought?**
I tie the security action to a physical action I already do automatically — closing my laptop lid the moment I stand up to leave my desk, rather than relying on remembering to lock it separately. Making it part of the same motion as leaving removes the chance of forgetting.

**What steps will you take to ensure your passwords and accounts are secure?**
Keep using a password manager (Apple's password manager) so every account has a strong, unique, generated password instead of one I reuse or can guess, unlock with fingerprint/Face ID rather than typing passwords in visible places, and progressively enable 2FA on every work account that supports it, not just GitHub.

**What would you do if you suspected a security breach or suspicious activity on your account?**
I would immediately change the password on the affected account, check and revoke any active sessions or connected apps/tokens I don't recognize, confirm 2FA is still enabled and hasn't been altered, and notify my mentor/IT right away rather than trying to handle it quietly on my own — early reporting limits the damage.
